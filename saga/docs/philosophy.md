# The philosophy of Habits Saga

Why Saga tracks what it tracks, why the attributes are the ones they are, and why the game keeps its workings to itself.

This document is for the people who make the game, and for any player curious about the thinking. How to play is in the [manual](manual.md). The story is in the [lore](lore.md).

## Part 1: Tracking habits

### Habits first

Habits are the point of Saga. A habit is something you do again and again, and it is the only thing that grows in the game: each one becomes a companion who levels up, learns and fights.

Tasks matter too, but less. A one-off task counts because it might become a habit. The person who calls the plumber once has done a chore. The person who keeps noticing what needs a call, and makes it, is building something. So tasks are spells: useful and satisfying, gone once cast, and they pay nothing. What they leave behind is a companion slightly better at casting.

### What playing asks of you

**Microimprovements.** Every blow counts. A single repetition is small and looks like nothing, and the game doesn't pretend otherwise: an early attack barely scratches a rat. It counts anyway, and the count stays.

**The compound effect.** A habit kept long enough becomes a powerful companion. Its level raises its strength, and its mastery multiplies that strength by a small share with each level. The curve is steep on purpose, so growth is slow at first and then large. A companion who has been with you for a year should feel unlike one who joined last week, because the habit behind it is.

**Game mechanics as motivators.** A wounded foe waiting at camp is a reason to do the habit today. A party one blow from victory is a reason to do one more. The game supplies a reason to act on the days your own reasons run out.

**Intentional lockouts and punishments.** Some things are meant to be hard or closed:

- A companion acts once per turn, however many times you did the habit.
- Once a battle starts, the only ways out are winning or fleeing.
- XP and gold come only from victories.
- A knocked-out companion can't act until someone revives them, so neglecting the party has a cost in what you can record.
- A task can only be cast by a companion of its school who has the MP for it.
- A vice takes attribute points, and they stay taken.

These are not obstacles to get around. They are where the game's meaning comes from, because a game that can't refuse you can't reward you either.

**Different areas of life, five at a time.** You may track as many areas as you like, but only five companions fight together. That limit is the philosophy in its plainest form: you can care about many things and still improve only a few at once. Choosing who is in the party is choosing what you are working on now.

### Trust

An attack means "I just did this habit", and nothing checks that you did. Saga runs on trust, and it stays that way. A cheat can reach the top of the Hall of Fame and will have gained nothing the game can give, because what the game gives is the habit. That is why an honest log comes first, always: even an attack that misses still records that you did the habit, because you did.

### Why these eight attributes

An attribute is not a score you assign. No screen lets you spend points. An attribute is a record: the total of what your companions in that area have become, minus what your vices have taken. To raise one, you have to go and live it.

The eight come in four pairs. Each pair splits one area of life at the place where people tend to do one half and neglect the other.

| Pair                  | First half                               | Second half                                 | Why they are separate                                                                                       |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The body              | **Body**: what moves it                  | **Vitality**: what maintains it             | Hard training on no sleep is a familiar failure. One does not stand in for the other.                       |
| The mind              | **Mind**: what you take in               | **Craft**: what you make                    | Reading about music is not making music. It is easy to take in forever and never put anything out.          |
| Inner and outer order | **Spirit**: the practice within          | **Order**: the house and affairs around you | A calm inner life with unpaid bills, or a spotless house with nothing inside: each can hide the other.      |
| Others and limits     | **Charisma**: what passes between people | **Resolve**: what you do beyond your limit  | One reaches outward and the other pushes through. Both need a person to do what is uncomfortable right now. |

**Resolve is the exception.** The other seven name an area of life. Resolve names a degree of effort: exceptional work, the extra hour, the thing you did past the point where you would normally stop. It used to mean only finished projects, which made it rare, since long projects seldom finish. Effort beyond the usual comes up far more often, and it is what the attribute was always about.

This leaves one rule of thumb. The same activity can belong to two attributes depending on how it is done. Writing a page a day is Craft. Staying at the desk for the page you didn't want to write is Resolve. The [manual](manual.md#the-eight-attributes) gives examples and a tie-break.

### Why each attribute has its school

A companion's spell school follows from what its attribute does for a life, so the battle reflects the habit.

| Attribute | School  | What it does in battle                      | Why it fits                                                                        |
| --------- | ------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Body      | Force   | Strikes the foe                             | The plainest thing strength does.                                                  |
| Vitality  | Mend    | Heals the whole party                       | Rest and food restore everyone you are, not one part of you.                       |
| Mind      | Insight | The foe's next turn does less damage        | Knowing what is coming takes the force out of it.                                  |
| Craft     | Anthem  | The party's attacks hit harder for the turn | What you make lifts others. It adds nothing by itself, and everything to the rest. |
| Spirit    | Purge   | Cures ailments and heals a little           | Ailments come from vices the purse couldn't pay for. Practice clears them away.    |
| Order     | Ward    | The party takes less damage for the turn    | A house in order is what absorbs the bad week.                                     |
| Resolve   | Finish  | Strikes harder the more wounded the foe is  | The last push is worth more than the first. Effort pays most near the end.         |
| Charisma  | Charm   | A chance that the foe loses its turn        | Some fights are won by not having them.                                            |

## Part 2: The game

### A game, not a gamified tracker

Saga is not a habit tracker with points added. It is a full game, a simple one, whose only way forward runs through your real life. Nor is it meant to be fun for its own sake, and that is less of a contradiction than it sounds: the game should be good, with real decisions and interesting dynamics, but it can only be played by doing the things you set out to do. If the game were fun without the habits, it would compete with them.

### Where it comes from

Saga is a turn-based RPG in the line of Final Fantasy and early-2000s games such as Legend of Legaia, with a mix of others in the genre. It borrows their shape: a party, turns, a road of ever harder foes, inns, potions, the fanfare at the end of a battle. It is not a spin-off of any of them. Those games understood that slow growth feels wonderful when you can see where it took you, and that is exactly what a habit needs.

### Obscure on purpose

**We do not aim at transparency.** The player doesn't need to know what happens behind the scenes. Things stay obscure and mystical so that exploring is rewarded: what does fighting do to a companion, what do the skills change, what happens if you run? You find out by playing, as you did with the games this one descends from.

What this means in practice:

- **The game's words belong to the story.** Labels speak in the voice of the [lore](lore.md). They don't explain anything that someone who has read the manual would already know.
- **The numbers of a fight stay on screen.** Damage, HP, MP, gold and levels are what make a battle readable, and a game needs them.
- **The reasoning behind them does not.** How XP is shared out, what the odds of escape are and how a vice's heat grows are left to be discovered.
- **Warnings stay.** Before anything irreversible, such as deleting a companion or a vice, or logging a vice, the game says what is about to happen. Mystery belongs to the game, and it never costs a player their progress by surprise.

The [manual](manual.md) sits outside the game for anyone who wants answers, with the workings in a separate part marked as spoilers. A small [in-game help](help.md) gives just enough to get going.

This also clears space. Every explanation that leaves a screen makes room for something to play with.
